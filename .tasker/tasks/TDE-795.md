Step 3. What makes something a STEP rather than a sub-part of a step? Current answer to measure against: 'the contract is the unit — a step earns task-hood only if it produces a distinct, checkable output a later step consumes; else it's a milestone' (137). Do you agree granularity is contract-driven, or should steps be defined some other way? Prevents 40-micro-task flows. OUTPUT = the granularity rule.

---

## OUTPUT — the granularity rule (settled 2026-07-27)

**Steps are located by what a boundary must DO, not by how big the work is.**

Promote a boundary to a step when it must do one of these four things — the only four things a boundary can do that a milestone cannot:
- **hand an artifact over** — something concrete exists that the next part consumes
- **change the executor** — a human does one part, an agent the next (the executor attribute lives on the step, so this is impossible inside one)
- **permit a gate** — a contract can only sit at a seam; no seam, no possible gate
- **permit a clean cold stop** — you could stop for the night and someone else resumes from written state alone

If a boundary does none of the four, it stays a **milestone inside a step**, however substantial the work is.

**Size is the calibration, not the locator.** If a step can't be held in one go without a context dump, look inside it for one of the four triggers. If there genuinely isn't one, the work is homogeneous batch work — and cutting by volume is legitimate there precisely because there is nothing else to cut on.

**Contracts don't create cuts; cuts may carry contracts.** Cut first (on handover / executor / stop / gate-ability), then decide per seam whether that seam is worth gating — and usually it isn't.

**Milestones on steps stay legal but are a smell** — a signal the flow is under-resolved and work is hiding inside a step that should have been visible as steps.

### Why this REVERSES the current rule
The shipped rule ("the contract is the unit — a step earns task-hood only if it produces a distinct checkable output a later step consumes", 137) promotes every checkable output to a step, pushing toward MANY steps. This rule pushes toward FEW. That is deliberate and follows from Q1: if the root problem is a wall of context, a 40-step flow just rebuilds the wall one altitude up. **The flow's own legibility is a constraint on how many steps it should have.** It also applies Q2's demotion concretely — under the old definition the contract justified the cut; now the cut justifies itself and gating is an optional per-seam add-on.

The economic asymmetry underneath it: a step is a real row with status, detail, dependency blocking and a possible gate; a milestone is one entry in an array. Milestones are cheap, steps are expensive, so the default is milestones and promotion must be earned.

### Findings that fall out of Q3

**Scale locates nothing.** Q1/Q2 settled that scale decides whether the whole operation is a flow — it says how MANY cuts are needed, never WHERE they fall. That was a conflation in the first pass at this question and it is now closed: goal/handover locates, size calibrates.

**Handoff ≠ gate.** Q2 demoted *gating* to optional; it did NOT demote the *handoff*. In almost any process step two starts from what step one left behind whether or not anyone checks it. Two different things were wearing one word ("contract"), which is why the old rule appeared to cover both.

**A step is a unit of work OR a unit of state — and this rule picks work.** The contract design implies state (the output is the point, the work incidental); Q1's scale definition implies work (the sitting is the point, the output incidental). Three senses of "step" were in play — unit of attention (capacity), unit of purpose (meaning), unit of accountability (state). They coincide on tidy work and come apart on exactly the big messy operations flows exist for. This rule takes attention/work as primary and treats accountability as one of four promotion triggers rather than the definition.

**Homogeneous batch vs heterogeneous process may be a real flow TYPE (feeds Q6).** Some big operations have no goal shift anywhere along their length (clean 300 tasks, migrate 80 files) — cuts there are arbitrary and size is the only locator. Others change character as they go. That distinction changes how you cut, whether templates apply, and where gating could even go, so it plausibly passes Q2's type razor. Operational test: **can you name the two chunks without numbering them?** If the only honest names are "part 1"/"part 2", there is no goal shift and you're in batch territory.

**Do flow steps need milestones? — mostly no, and it's a diagnostic.** Outside a flow, milestones are how a task exposes internal structure (the detail-vs-milestones directive). Inside a flow that job is already taken twice: the step list carries the sequence and the contract's rules carry the "what must be true" checklist. So if steps routinely need milestones, the steps are too big — the fix is to cut the step, not to add them.
